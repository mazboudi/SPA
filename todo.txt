Team,


Thank you for the work that has gone into the Software Delivery Automation initiative to date. We have reviewed the current direction with the broader EUC leadership and engineering teams and wanted to provide consolidated feedback.

 

We remain supportive of modernization and automation where it delivers measurable business value. However, we continue to have concerns that the current solution does not yet demonstrate how it addresses the primary operational challenges we are trying to solve.

 

1. The primary opportunity remains the software request and intake experience


From an EUC perspective, our largest challenge today is not software packaging itself, but the software request and intake process.

Today, users navigate multiple forms, portals, and manual workflows to request software. They are often required to provide information that already exists elsewhere, submit supporting documentation separately, or request software that is already packaged and available through the Intune Company Portal. In addition, requests are not consistently validated against approved and denied software catalogs, which can lead to duplicate requests, unnecessary effort, and additional cycle time.

 

As a result, we believe the greatest opportunity for improvement lies in simplifying and modernizing the request experience and overall intake process. While we understand that intake-related capabilities are part of the roadmap, we have not yet seen an end-to-end demonstration that shows how the future-state experience will materially improve these challenges.

 

This remains the area where we believe the project can deliver the most meaningful business value.

 

2. The ServiceNow integration workstream requires further demonstration


We understand there has been significant effort around ServiceNow integration, catalog management, and governance workflows. However, EUC has not yet seen a complete demonstration of the intended end-state experience or the operational benefits these integrations are expected to provide.

 

Because catalog integration is central to reducing duplicate requests, improving user guidance, and streamlining governance, we would like to see a walkthrough that demonstrates:

Validation against approved and denied software catalogs.
The user experience when software is already packaged and available.
The user experience when requested software is denied and alternatives exist.
The workflow for requesting net-new software.
The manual steps that will be removed from the current EUC and HCL processes.

Seeing the end-to-end workflow will help us better understand how these capabilities improve the current process and where measurable efficiencies will be gained.


3. We have questions regarding the long-term supportability and operational model of the packaging factory


While automation within the packaging process may provide value, we have concerns regarding the long-term supportability, sustainability, and operational complexity of the proposed packaging factory.

 

Our current understanding is that the solution contains a significant amount of customization. As we evaluate the long-term operating model, we would like a clearer understanding of:

The support model following implementation.
Knowledge transfer and documentation plans.
Ongoing maintenance requirements.
Ownership responsibilities after project transition.
How future enhancements and troubleshooting will be performed.

Separately, packaging execution itself is not currently the primary bottleneck in our process. The larger constraints we experience today are typically related to:

Intake quality and completeness.
Obtaining installers and source files.
Governance and approval activities.
Testing and validation coordination.

Because of this, we would like to better understand how the packaging factory contributes to overall process improvements and how it addresses the areas that drive the majority of our current cycle time.


4. The target-state operating model and business value need to be better defined


One of the key questions that remains is what the future-state operating model looks like and what work is actually being eliminated.

 

Our understanding today is that HCL would still be required for significant portions of the packaging and deployment process. If that understanding is correct, we would like to understand:

What activities are being automated or removed.
Which teams will perform less work as a result.
What reduction in cycle time is expected.
What operational efficiencies are anticipated.
What support structure will exist after transition.
 

These are important inputs in helping us evaluate the overall business value of the solution and its impact on day-to-day operations.

 

Summary

 

To be clear, our concern is not with automation itself. We support investments that simplify processes, reduce operational effort, improve the user experience, and deliver measurable outcomes.

 

At this stage, however, we have not yet seen a clear demonstration that the proposed solution:

 

Simplifies the end-user software request experience.
Integrates approved and denied software catalogs into the request workflow.
Reduces duplicate or unnecessary requests.
Removes manual effort from EUC and HCL processes.
Reduces overall cycle time.
Delivers a sustainable and supportable operating model.
Produces measurable business outcomes beyond technical automation.
We would welcome a detailed walkthrough of the intended end-state design and operating model so that the team can better understand how these objectives will be achieved.

Thank you, and we look forward to continuing the discussion.

 SPA Framework/Workbench Benefits (Windows) 

The Software Package Automation (SPA) framework and the front-end packager “Workbench” standardizes, organizes, simplifies, and accelerates the creation of Win32 apps in Microsoft Intune. The Workbench brings back some of the functionality lost when moving from Microsoft Configuration Manager (aka SCCM) to Microsoft Intune, like the ability to clone existing app packages while the SPA framework automates tedious processes such as building a .intunewin file and uploading it to Intune. The latest version of the PowerShell App Deployment Toolkit (PSADT) is fully integrated into the Workbench. PSADT deployment templates are automatically generated. The Workbench’s PSADT Visual Action Builder allows packagers to construct deployment scripts for simple applications in minutes while providing functionality to construct extremely complicated deployment scripts where necessary. The Workbench utilizes GitLab to store all information about app packages. A single GitLab project can be used to store successive versions of Intune app packages. Packagers can easily create new GitLab projects or commit changes to existing ones using the Workbench. A project need not have all the necessary information to create an Intune app. Simply committing changes acts as a save button, allowing packagers to pick up where they left off. Using Intune by itself to build Win32 apps is an extremely rigid process. Every required field must be filled out and the .intunewin file uploaded before the Win32 app can be created. Not so with the Workbench. Projects can be created with minimal data to begin with, allowing packagers to gradually develop all the necessary pieces of the Intune Win32 app package in the Workbench. Once all those necessary pieces are in place, the Workbench can create a new Intune app or update an existing with one click. Intune by itself is akin to a no-frills sports car with a manual transmission. Intune + Workbench (and underlying SPA framework) is akin to a self-driving luxury sedan with all the optional features. 

Standardization Improvements 

The Workbench can import existing Intune app metadata and associated PSADT deployment script, making it easy to bring existing apps into the SPA paradigm. Older versions of PSADT scripts are automatically converted to the latest standard (currently v4.1.8). 

When no existing Intune app/PSADT script exists, the Workbench automatically generates new “scaffolding” and guides the packager through every step of the app creation process. Packagers are unable to utilize old versions of PSADT templates. 

Although the use of PSADT is mandated for Intune Win32 apps at Fiserv, the Workbench forces the packager to use the latest approved version with EUC-approved default configurations, branding, etc. 

Organizational Improvements 

The use of GitLab to store project information allows packagers to better organize different versions or revisions of the same application. For example, a single GitLab project (seamlessly accessed from the Workbench) can store app package data for version 1.0, 1.1, 1.2, etc. Perhaps there are multiple test/development packages for version 1.0. They can be stored in the same project as version 1.0 rev1, rev2, rev3, etc. 

Collaboration is simplified since all app package information is stored in GitLab. One packager can start work on a specific version of an app package in the Workbench and commit changes. Another packager can then open the same version for editing. There is no question as to what the first packager may have done. It’s all visible through the Workbench. No one needs to ask where the latest PSADT deployment script is on the file share or go searching for it. Except for installation files/binaries, everything is stored in GitLab. The location of installation files and associated support files is stored in the GitLab project as well. 

Simplification of Intune Packaging Workflow 

The Workbench steps packagers through each phase of the packaging workflow in a deliberate fashion for net new app packages.  

Select New Title and enter GitLab project information 

Provide location of installation files. 

Add any additional actions/custom code (if necessary) to the automatically generated PSADT scaffolding via the Visual Action Builder. 

Supply Intune metadata via a UI that closely resembles Intune’s own Win32 app creation UI. 

Commit changes to GitLab only, build PSADT template and download artifacts for testing, or publish to Intune (and optionally assign to test groups). 

If a GitLab project already exists for a specific version of an Intune app package, the process follows a similar path. If a packager wishes to make edits to an existing version: 

Select Edit Title and choose the desired version from the project selection screen. Verify GitLab project information. 

Verify (or update) location of installation files. 

Make changes to PSADT deployment script via Visual Action builder if necessary. 

Make changes to Intune metadata if necessary. 

Commit changes to GitLab only, build PSADT template and download artifacts for testing, or publish to Intune (and optionally assign to test groups). When publishing to Intune in this scenario, the existing Intune app associated with the specific project version is automatically deleted and recreated. 

If the packager wants to create a new version of an existing application: 

Select Clone Title and choose the desired version from the project selection screen. Supply only new project version if creating within the same GitLab project or rename project to generate entirely new GitLab project. 

Provide location of installation files. 

Make changes to PSADT deployment script via Visual Action builder where necessary. 

Make changes to Intune metadata where necessary. 

Commit changes to GitLab only, build PSADT template and download artifacts for testing, or publish to Intune (and optionally assign to test groups). When publishing to Intune in this scenario, a new app is created in Intune. 

 

Acceleration/automation of Intune App Creation  

Generation of the PSADT deployment template for testing is an automated process. The packager simply selects the “Build PSADT” option and waits for the process to complete. The build artifacts used for testing become available to download on the same screen once the process is completed. 

Publishing an app from the Workbench (once all the necessary information has been supplied) is completely automated. The .intunewin file is always generated automatically. Packagers are no longer required to follow the manual process outlined in Prepare Win32 App Content for Upload. 

Microsoft Configuration Manager allowed packagers to clone an existing app package. Intune does not have this functionality, but the Workbench does. One of the most common packaging exercises is creating a new version of an app that has already been packaged. This is a tedious process in Intune. A packager would need to do the following to create a new version of an existing Intune app: 

Find the location of the PSADT template used in the previous app version. This template (basically a folder with a bunch of scripts, config files, and installation files) is not connected to the Intune app in any way. Usually a packager will reference the location in the Intune app’s notes section. 

Delete the contents of the Files folder within the PSADT template (possibly the SupportFiles folder too) and replace with the new version’s installer file(s). 

Edit the PSADT deployment script to account for the new version as necessary. If the deployment template is outdated, conversion to the latest PSADT template is not straightforward or user friendly. As such, packagers most likely avoid this whenever possible and continue to use outdated PSADT deployment templates. 

Manually generate a new .intunewin file from the PSADT deployment template. 

Open Intune in two different browser windows. Open the existing Win32 app to be “cloned” in one window and initiate the process to create a new Win32 app in the other. Upload the .intunewin file to start the creation process and then begin to copy and paste all the information/settings from the existing app into the new app, changing relevant sections to reflect the new app version being created. 

To edit an existing app’s underlying PSADT deployment script (or any part of the PSADT template such as a config file in the Files folder), a packager would need to manually rebuild the .intunewin file and upload to the existing Intune app. 

In comparison to the lengthy, tedious processes required to “clone” an existing app or edit an existing app’s PSADT template, the Workbench has simple Edit Title and Clone Title options as described in the Simplification of Intune Packaging Workflow section. 

Importing an existing Intune app’s metadata and associated PSADT deployment script into the Workbench to create a new project is also a very quick and painless process. Eventually this Intune/PSADT import option won’t be necessary, but it saves an enormous amount of time for any packager wanting to convert legacy PSADT folder templates to current version standards. The conversion capability of the Workbench is far superior to PSADT’s conversion utilities and much easier to use. It’s unlikely packagers even convert old PSADT 3.x templates to the latest version (4.1.8) because of the tedious, time-consuming work involved. The Workbench’s conversion capabilities have been tested on the most complicated PSADT deployment scripts. It offers extremely fast and robust conversion capability.

Executive Summary
What the MVP was for, and where it stops.
The MVP was intended to replace EUC’s existing manual software request process: to make it faster, and to show a requester what stage their request had reached. That intent is sound and the underlying need is real.
The purpose of the MVP is version specificity. The question EUC needs answered at request time is not whether a piece of software is permitted, it is whether the version being asked for is permitted. Titles are approved with a floor, “this version and above,” because older builds carry vulnerabilities, fall out of support, or reach end of life. That judgement is the value the MVP adds. Approving a title while saying nothing about which version may run leaves EUC where it is today.
 
The finding
The primary requirement cannot be expressed in ServiceNow’s native software model.
Governance in the platform attaches to a title. The decision EUC needs attaches to a version. Those are different grains, and the one place the platform carries a version condition supports only four operators: is, starts with, is anything, and is empty. There is no greater than anywhere in the model.
So a rule reading “approved at 18.0.0 and above” has no native representation. This is not a question of data volume or of process. The platform governs at title level, the requirement sits at version level, and the platform offers no operator at that level to express it.
Everything else in this document follows from that.
The specific problem with EUC’s lists
The lists live in SharePoint. Loading them into a table in ServiceNow is straightforward. Turning them into software models is not, and that distinction is the whole of the problem.
A software model must be tied to a specific publisher and a specific product that the platform already recognises, and it refuses to save when it cannot be. Neither identifier is in the list, and neither can be derived from it, because deciding what a piece of software is and who publishes it is a judgment rather than a lookup.
That judgment has to be made for every title before a single model exists, and even then the model would carry the title rather than the version rule the MVP exists to enforce.
What that means for the timeline
The indication given in the September 3rd meeting was that work of this kind runs to six to nine months and is resource intensive. That is not a costed estimate for EUC’s population and should not be quoted as one. It is the only figure anyone at Fiserv has offered for work of this type, from people who have done it, and it is the right order of magnitude to plan against.
Six to nine months of prerequisite work does not fit inside the fourteen week delivery plan, and that work has not started. Adding development resource does not change this, because the prerequisite is not development work.
Two further conclusions
The original design cannot be carried forward. Too many of the assumptions it rests on turned out not to hold once we were building against a real instance with real data. Those are set out in section 2.
A governed catalog is a solution with a delivery timeline, and it also requires a solution for owning and maintaining it afterwards. Titles change constantly, through new versions, new publishers, end of life, licence changes, and denials that need alternatives. Every new title carries the same judgement about publisher and product, and every new release moves a version floor. Building it and keeping it accurate are two commitments, and both need to be planned.
A design can specify the first. The second is an organisational commitment that has to sit alongside it, and it is worth settling before further build work is commissioned.
There is, however, a smaller approach that does address the primary requirement. Section 6 sets out a proposal raised in a recent review with the Alldyn team. It is the only route identified that expresses a version rule at all, and it should be read alongside everything below.
Where this comes from
The foundation of the scoped application was complete, along with the design and logic of the simplified request form, and the work had moved on to assembling a working example using software titles taken from the list published on the FTS Front Door. Attempting to represent those titles as software models is what exposed the constraints described in this document, and it led to a closer review of how approved software is already operationalised by the server and mainframe group.
That review led to a meeting on September 3rd, hosted by John Bradley, with the group that built and runs the existing approved and denied software solution. Robert Cha, who has been involved in every aspect of that work, set out what it took. Three points bear directly on EUC’s plan:
•	A significant effort, by a team, sustained over several years. The solution has been in place and actively managed throughout.
•	Version handling proved unmanageable and had to be constrained rather than solved. They met the same wall and worked around it.
•	Flexera and ServiceNow do not identify software the same way, so overlapping publisher and product models had to be reconciled title by title.
1. Why this is a data problem before it is a process one
This looks like a workflow problem. It is not. It is a data problem, and the workflow is only the visible part of it.
Every step in the intake process is a lookup, not a decision. The process does not decide anything. It reads decisions someone already made and recorded, on records someone maintains.
The step appears to	What it actually does
Find your software	Search a catalog of governed titles, which has to exist first
Determine whether your version is approved	Read a version rule someone recorded and keeps current as releases ship
Determine whether it is approved	Read a decision already recorded against that title
Offer an approved alternative	Follow a link someone created and keeps current
Establish whether a licence is needed	Read entitlement data maintained elsewhere
Check whether a package exists	Read a record the packaging process keeps up to date
Not one of those data sets exists in governed form today. The approved and denied lists are documents written for people to read. They work because whoever reads them already knows what the list does not say: that “Adobe Systems”, “Adobe Software” and “Adobe Inc” all refer to the same publisher, and that one of them has to be selected as the canonical publisher for any given product; who to ask about a title; why something was denied. A system cannot infer any of that, and it cannot go and ask.
This is what makes the data the substance of the work. The request process can be built in weeks. It will not return an answer to anyone until the data behind it exists and someone owns the review and validity of the values in ServiceNow.
That is why the solution needs an owner as well as a delivery date. Software, once built, continues to work as it was built; data does not hold still. New versions ship, approved floors move up behind them, and decisions are revisited as support and vulnerability positions change. Keeping the catalog accurate is a continuing responsibility rather than a state reached at handover.
Could the routing and approvals be built now, and the data connected later?
It is a fair question, and the difficulty is that the approved and denied lists are themselves the governance baseline. Where a request matches an approved title at a version within bounds, the decision has already been made and no review is required; the request goes straight to fulfilment. Governance initiates only where there is no match, where the version falls outside the approved range, or where the title is denied or still under evaluation.
Without that data in place nothing can confirm that a request is already approved. Every request therefore routes to evaluation, including for software approved years ago, carrying installer capture, security review, a governance task and the creation of a new record. The process would not sit idle waiting for data; it would run continuously and send everything down the longest path.
The routing also cannot be designed ahead of the data. Whether governance attaches to a title or to a specific version determines what the process receives, what it branches on, and how many tasks it raises. Building first means building against an assumption and rebuilding once the answer is known.
The approvals present the same difficulty from another direction. Which groups own the security, legal, architecture, licensing and packaging decisions is an open question, and a process cannot route to groups that have not been named.
There is a practical risk as well in putting a working looking request path in front of people before it can answer anything. Requests will be submitted to it, and a path that leads nowhere is harder to withdraw than one that was never opened.
Data first, then process. Not as a matter of preference, but because the process has nothing to act on until the data can answer.
2. Why the original design cannot be used
The design was correct for the platform behaviour it assumed, and the assumption was a reasonable one: that a version recorded against a software product model would persist as written, and that the record would hold the governance decision placed on it.
In practice those records are not inert storage. They are the target of SAM Pro’s normalization, and the platform actively maintains them. The model’s identity is composed from manufacturer, product, version and edition by a business rule that fires on insert and not on update. The manufacturer is silently replaced with its canonical company on every write. A save is aborted outright where the product’s publisher does not resolve to the same company as the model’s manufacturer. Drop the product and the title disappears from the computed name.
The table looks like a place to record a governed decision about a version. It behaves as a normalization target, and the distinction only becomes visible once you write to it. That is the assumption underneath the design, and it is the one that did not hold.
Each of the following emerged during the build, and each removes something the design was resting on.
Version rules cannot be expressed in the platform. This is the one that matters most, because version specificity is the purpose of the MVP. The approved list says “this version and above.” ServiceNow’s version condition supports only is, starts with, is anything and is empty. There is no greater than operator anywhere in the software model, and governance in the platform attaches to a title rather than to a version in any case. The central requirement has no native representation, so it has to be designed rather than configured.
There is no governed catalog of approved software to point the request form at. It has to be assembled title by title first.
The approved and denied lists cannot seed that catalog. Creating a software record requires tying each title to a specific publisher and a specific product the platform already recognises. Where those do not exist, someone has to decide what the software is and who publishes it, a judgement the lists do not contain and a script cannot make.
The request form is specified by this project, not built by it. Catalog items go through a separate formal intake: a requirements workbook completed and submitted to the catalog standards team, who build and maintain the item. Defining the form is ours; producing it is theirs. That applies regardless of who does the surrounding technical work, and it carries a consequence worth spelling out.
The workbook is a form definition document. It captures the questions, their answer types, and simple visibility rules of the form “show this field when that one is filled in.” It has no place to describe a field whose behaviour depends on a lookup against the software catalog, or a form that reshapes itself according to what that lookup returns. That behaviour is most of what makes the simplified form work: it stays short because it reveals only what the requester’s own answers make relevant, and it can only do that by asking the platform questions as the requester goes.
The simplified form presents two fields when it loads: the software title, and an option for software that is not listed. Everything else appears only when a requester’s answers call for it.
The request form is a catalog item, and catalog items are built and maintained by a separate team from the one designing the process. Specifying that behaviour to a level where that team could reproduce it, and then maintain it afterwards as the process changes, would require documentation well beyond what the intake workbook provides for, and arguably beyond what a catalog creator or manager should reasonably be asked to carry.
The existing Software Request form is the evidence for how this lands. It presents all of its fields at once rather than revealing them as they become relevant, which is what a form specified through a document of that kind will tend to look like. This is not a remark about the catalog team, who build accurately to the specification they are handed. It is that the specification format cannot express the design, so the simplification is the thing most likely to be lost in the handoff, and the result would be another long form of the sort the redesign set out to replace.
Work tracked in Development does not flow forward on its own. Stories belong in Production, where a defined process calls development work into a release cycle. Promotion between environments rides that same process.
There is not a single front door. More than one software catalog is live in Production, each with its own way in. Duplication is the smaller half of that problem. The larger one is that EUC titles and server titles could carry conflicting approved or denied status, and different governance directives, for the same software. Nothing today prevents that, and any new design would have to address it deliberately.
Individually these are solvable. Together they mean the target state cannot be reached from the position the platform and the data are currently in.
3. What any future attempt would require
An owner and a standing team. The group behind the existing solution has one, and has actively managed it for several years. That is the clearest available evidence of what this takes to keep working, not just to stand up.
The MVP was scoped here as a seven sprint build and is, on the evidence, a data and governance undertaking with a technical component, and the technical component is the smaller half.
What the existing solution shows
Fiserv already has a working software approval solution, owned and actively managed by another team, with its own request path. Their scope is server and mainframe. Whether it covers or could be extended to the workstation population is worth asking them directly, and would change the shape of anything new.
It is worth describing, because it is a thorough piece of work and it demonstrates the point of this document better than anything we built.
All approval statuses are held on a single custom table which that team actively maintains. Each row carries reference fields pointing to the software model in the CMDB, and to the corresponding product and publisher records in the platform’s software content tables. Their request form then uses a cascading filter: the requester selects a publisher first, and the list of software titles narrows to that publisher’s titles.
That is a considered design. It addresses the findability problem directly, by reducing the search space before the requester types anything, and it uses the platform’s own content structure rather than working around it.
It also shows where the limit sits. Publisher records are not one per company. A single company can appear under more than one publisher name, for example Adobe Inc and Adobe Systems, and the titles attach to only one of them. A requester who picks the wrong variant is shown an empty list for a publisher whose software is fully approved. The form is built correctly. The data model underneath it permits two reasonable answers to “who publishes this,” and no amount of form design resolves that for the requester.
This is the clearest evidence available for the point made at the start of this document. A solution that is well designed, actively staffed, and years into operation still meets the same constraint, because the constraint lives in how software identity is represented and governed, not in how any individual form is built. Any new effort would meet it in the same place.
Their form filters by publisher, then by title, and stops. It does not ask which version the requester needs, and it does not evaluate one. Version compliance is managed later, by finding what is installed and remediating it. That is a coherent choice, and it is a different problem from the one EUC is trying to solve, which is to stop the wrong version being requested in the first place.
What a replacement has to accommodate
The existing process is documented in the IT Software Asset Management SOP, and the frustration with it is what this engagement was commissioned to address. That is not in dispute. But anything replacing it still has to work inside what the SOP already fixes, and those are design constraints rather than open choices:
•	Governance is version aware, and the platform is not. The SOP has whole sections on approved software versions, version management and version remediation. That is where the actual control lives, and it is handled today after installation, through remediation and risk acceptance, rather than at the point of request. Anything built has to decide which of those two moments it acts at.
•	Three statuses, not four. Approved, Denied, and Under Evaluation. A title on neither list is Under Evaluation by definition, which is Fiserv’s name for the state the design referred to as “not found.” It is a governed state with a defined meaning rather than a gap.
•	A required Technology Asset Owner per title, accountable for keeping versions aligned to standards. The role is defined corporately. Whether owners have been assigned across EUC’s titles is not something we established, and it bears on any design that routes to them.
•	All approver groups must approve. A single group declining marks the title Denied. Any routing design has to reflect that, including the failure path.
•	A time limited exception route. Temporary Software Approval, requiring SVP approval before submission, with Cyber Risk Management granting three to twelve months and no more. The design treated exceptions as a governance branch. In practice they are a separate instrument with an expiry, which is a difference worth carrying into anything new.
•	Enforcement already exists at the endpoint. Controls in the FEAD.ONE domain block denied and under evaluation software from executing, and block manual installs from unapproved sources. A request process does not need to enforce anything, and should not try.
None of this makes the original intent wrong. It does mean a replacement is a redesign of a governed process with existing statuses, owners, approval rules and enforcement, rather than a new process on open ground.
The boundary any new design must respect
A design that resolves EUC’s titles to software models would be writing into the same shared platform records the existing solution depends on, including identifiers deliberately retained from the retired Flexera system. That is a boundary to be deliberate about rather than to discover during a migration.
An approach that holds EUC’s data in its own table, as section 6 describes, largely avoids this. It touches the shared model records only where a title is deliberately linked to one, which is optional and can be done a row at a time rather than as a bulk load.
4. Why this surfaced when it did
Three things set the sequence, and none of them were avoidable.
The order the work had to be done in
Discovery against the development instance ran in late June, and admin access sufficient to build, meaning scoped applications, tables, catalog items and flows, was settled by mid July. Foundation work began immediately. That order was not optional. The design required the application architecture, its tables and its security layer to exist before anything could be built on top of them, and that architecture then had to be preserved through an upgrade whose impact was not yet known.
The constraints became visible only once we began creating software model records against real titles. The first of those was August 18th. Nothing before that point required a software model to exist, so nothing before that point could have surfaced the problem.
Some of it was not discoverable from the instance at all. What the existing approved and denied solution took to build, and what its owners learned in doing it, sits with another team. That came from a conversation rather than from anything visible in the data.
A platform defect removed source control for the entire build
Foundation work began knowing an environment upgrade was pending, which made it important that everything built could be preserved and reapplied afterwards.
Connecting the application to its GitLab repository failed. The cause was identified as a known ServiceNow defect (ServiceNow PRB1873250, KB0855846) affecting the patch level the Development instance was running, and it was fixed only in the release the instance later upgraded to. The practical effect was that source control, the normal mechanism for preserving and moving work, was unavailable for the whole build.
Every change therefore had to be tracked and exported by hand, and a significant amount of effort went into making sure what was built would survive an upgrade that had not happened yet.
The upgrade was a rebuild, and it introduced a second defect
The Development instance was not upgraded in place. It was rebuilt, and returned on August 13th as effectively a new instance. The entire application had to be restored from those hand made exports, which is the work the earlier effort had been insurance against.
The upgrade then introduced a second known platform defect (ServiceNow PRB2075293, KB3150595), affecting the automatic numbering of newly created records. It was reported as in progress in late August with no committed fix date and remains unresolved. Because it affects record creation, it threatens the submission step itself, and preparation had to continue on the basis that another rebuild or restore might be necessary.
None of this was scoped work. It is where several weeks went, and it is the reason the build reached the point of creating real software records in late August rather than early August.
5. What was built
Built, on the development instance: a scoped application with two supporting tables, a security layer, and a working request form. The form lets a requester find a software title, shows a governance decision where one has been recorded, offers an approved alternative when a title is denied, and creates a request linked to the selected record.
It runs against six software models created by hand for the purpose. Several of those have since lost the version and display name they were given, because those values are composed by a business rule that fires on insert and not on update, so opening and saving a record clears them. That is a demonstration of the constraint described in section 2 rather than a defect in the build, but it means the sample data is no longer in the state it was authored in.
Not reached: the filter restricting the form to governed titles was planned for Sprint 1 and not built, so selection is currently unconstrained. Nothing evaluates, routes, approves or records a governance outcome; that was the following sprint. There is no catalog behind the form. This is stated plainly because the form demonstrates well and it would be easy to take it for more than it is.
Nothing has been promoted. Everything is on the development instance. Nothing exists in Test or Production, and nothing is in front of any user.
6. An alternative worth pursuing
Proposed by Dean in a recent review with the Alldyn team. It is the most promising idea anyone has put forward and it deserves a proper look.
Stop requiring a software model before a request can be created. Load EUC’s approved and denied lists into a flat table within the scoped application, and point the request form at that instead.
The reason this matters is not convenience, it is that this is the only identified way to express a version rule at all. Held as text in a column, “18.0.0 and above” is a rule the platform will store and a script can evaluate. Held as a native software model it cannot be represented, because there is no greater than operator and governance attaches to the title rather than the version. The flat table is not a shortcut around the data problem, it is the only route to the primary requirement.
It is a simpler approach rather than a more sophisticated one, and that is the whole of its merit. Capturing a request, routing approval for a title that carries no disposition, and routing an exception request do not require a ServiceNow software model to be associated with the EUC list first. Once that association stops being a precondition:
•	Selection works. The requester picks from the real list rather than from six hand made records.
•	Findability is straightforward. Publisher, title and category are plain text, so ordinary contains search and category browsing work. The problem where “Acrobat” fails to match “Adobe Systems Acrobat DC Professional” does not arise, because nothing is composing a name from parts.
•	Version becomes expressible. “This version and above” is text in a column, displayed to the requester and compared in script at submission. This is the point of the whole approach rather than a side benefit.
•	Fulfillment routing is already in the data. “How to obtain” and “Available” carry Intune, Self Service, Ariba, and the Software Center access tier.
There is precedent for this inside Fiserv. The existing approved software solution is built on the same pattern: a flat table, with references to software models added incrementally rather than required up front. Rather more than half their rows now carry one, and their request path has worked throughout. They did not treat model resolution as a prerequisite, and neither does this.
Where this acts in the lifecycle
Version compliance across Fiserv is generally managed after installation, by finding what is running and remediating it. EUC’s approach is deliberately proactive: to restrain an unapproved version from being installed in the first place, rather than to find and replace it afterwards. That is a reasonable position, particularly where the endpoint controls already exist to enforce it, and this approach is what would let a request act on it.
The requester states the version they need. The system compares it against the approved constraint on the row and returns a decision at the point of request:
Row status	Version entered	Outcome
Approved	meets the constraint	Proceeds normally
Approved	meets the constraint, no current package	Proceeds, marked as requiring packaging before fulfilment
Approved	below the constraint	Proceeds as an exception request, flagged for review
Denied	any	Shows the approved alternative, with the option to request an exception
Under Evaluation	any	Offers an exception request against the version under review, or routes to the intake path with the information the knowledge article requires
No matching row	any	Offers free text entry of publisher, title and version, and routes to intake as a title carrying no disposition
any	constraint not machine readable	Routes to a person, rather than guessing
The exception routes matter because they map onto Temporary Software Approval, which already exists and is documented. Nothing is being invented.
This is illustrative rather than a finished decision table. The full set of outcomes, and the wording presented to a requester in each, would need to be worked through with the people who own the governance decisions.
This is also what distinguishes EUC’s requirement from what the existing solution provides. That form filters by publisher and then by title, and does not ask which version is wanted, because version compliance is handled downstream through remediation and risk acceptance. Deciding at request time is a different control at a different point in the lifecycle. It is not a better one in the abstract, but it is the one EUC’s approach depends on.
What it gives up, and what it does not
It gives up CMDB linkage, so no license position, no entitlement reconciliation, no discovery matching. That was never in the MVP scope, and carrying a nullable software model reference on the table from the first day leaves the path open, which is what the other team did.
It does not remove the governance question. Someone still has to own keeping the list current, and without that it becomes a fourth stale list rather than a working one. What changes is that governance stops being a prerequisite and becomes an ongoing responsibility, which is a far easier thing to staff.
What would need to be established first
1.	The version data has to be consistent enough to parse. This is the single question the approach stands or falls on, since version evaluation is the purpose. “18.0.0 and above” against a dotted version works. “Any supported version”, blank, or free text does not. Profiling that column across the full list is roughly an hour of work, and the distribution it returns decides whether this is straightforward, partial, or not viable. Do that before anything else.
2.	How versions are expressed, and whether those expressions align. The approved and denied lists record a version in their own format. Software install and discovery records in ServiceNow record it in theirs, and the two do not necessarily agree. This has no bearing on a request, which compares what the requester enters against what the list says. It becomes a factor when installed versions are reconciled against the EUC list, or when list rows are resolved to discovered software, and it is cheaper to establish now than during that reconciliation.
3.	Who owns the list once it is in ServiceNow, and how it stays aligned with the SharePoint source.
4.	Whether the request form is built by this project or through the catalog intake process, which is the constraint described in section 2 and applies regardless of what sits behind the form.
What this describes is a route to the decision the rest of the design was built around: whether this version of this software may be installed, answered at the moment someone asks. The form that asks it would still be built through the catalog intake process, as any request form would.
